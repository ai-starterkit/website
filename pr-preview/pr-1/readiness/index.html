AI Maturity Assessment

To understand how ready your institution is to use AI, look at your technical capabilities and at how prepared people are to change the way they work.

Start with what you want to improve for students and how you will measure success, including when you are improving operations behind the scenes. Involve colleagues across campus, with support from your leadership, and keep in mind that people in different roles may need different preparation. Check whether you can access reliable data securely and connect the systems you need. Finish with concrete next steps, whether you can begin a project now or need to address gaps first. Below, you can assess your college, university or system as a whole, or look closely at a particular area or proposed project.

Assess your institution overall

These are a small set of openly accessible assessments, chosen with broad-access institutions in mind. Use them to look at readiness across departments and decide where to focus. When comparing options, consider how the developers tested their approach and how much time your team would need to complete it. Look for practical guidance on involving colleagues and turning findings into an institutional plan, and check the permissions before adapting any materials.

Examine a specific area or project

You may already know where you might use AI and want a closer look at what it would take. In teaching and learning, for example, you could check how prepared instructors are to use AI in their courses and what support they still need. You could also add a readiness check to your project management office's approval process: does the proposed project need AI, and what must be in place before it goes ahead? This section will collect resources for these focused reviews.

Case studies

How institutions are building their readiness for AI.

Use case

University of North Carolina System Office

Where they started

In the University of North Carolina System Office, employees had received Copilot training and access to ChatGPT. Early adopters began proposing ways to automate manual tasks and improve longstanding processes, including in projects that had previously lacked resources. Employees also brought assumptions about what AI could do, while central AI staff saw opportunities to improve processes through conventional automation, changes in how work was organized, AI, or a combination of these.

This energy was hard to harness. People brought project ideas to different System Office staff separately, through informal conversations. System Office staff needed to understand both local technical capabilities and people's willingness and ability to adopt AI.

What changed

After introducing Copilot, System Office staff reviewed usage reports and found a small group of heavy users, while many employees seldom used the tool or did not know how to begin. Staff responded with regular learning sessions, webinars, a Teams channel and demonstrations by experienced users.

UNC's AI leadership had identified some adoption needs after introducing the technology, and concluded that a readiness assessment was needed, one that included a plan for helping employees use AI in their daily work.

The next step was to connect the intended benefits of AI to the UNC System's student success goals, including better learning, financial outcomes and student experiences. Even an administrative project should help employees spend less time on routine tasks and more time supporting students and institutions.

What's next

Within the System Office, staff are preparing to start a two-hour meeting every other week with representatives from the AI, project management, IT and data teams. Employees proposing projects will explain their problems directly to the group, and participants will ask questions, consider scope and identify what AI, if any, is really needed, always in the context of solving problems. Employees will not need to arrive with a technical solution already designed: the System Office's AI leadership will act as an enabling function to get projects off the ground.

For campus-led projects, the System Office is hiring an AI enablement partner to connect institutional staff with central expertise and resources. Meanwhile, a separate group is developing an open-source platform where campus teams can build projects using different AI models and share their work for others to reuse. Institutions will own and implement their projects locally, with the enablement partner helping them move from an idea to a working project.

The UNC System is also developing a systemwide community of practice to help campus leaders share examples, get practical guidance and hold productive conversations about AI with faculty, staff and students. Further plans include a senior director focused on AI in teaching and learning, and a public website featuring campus and System Office success stories.

Throughout this work, UNC emphasizes communication between leadership and the people responsible for implementation: technical staff need to understand leaders' expectations and have the resources to act.
