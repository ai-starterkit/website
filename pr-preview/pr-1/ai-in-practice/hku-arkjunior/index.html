The University of Hong Kong · X-Lab Shanghai

ArkJunior: A Learning Loop for Self-Explaining Lectures

  • Stage: 3-4 Piloting & Capability Building / Transition & Investment Planning
  • Scope: Teaching, Learning, and Assessment Strategies
  • Scale: Two-course pilot, moving to institution-wide platform

What it is

ArkJunior is a four-stage learning loop built from a course’s lecture materials. It turns lecture slides and notes into self-explaining lectures, while students’ questions, difficulties, and progress generate data that informs future teaching. Authoring (Studio) creates narrated lectures with progressive slide reveals and comprehension checks. Delivering (Player) lets students pause, ask questions, and receive answers grounded only in the course materials. Tutoring (Motu) is an assignable Moodle tutoring layer currently at the MVP stage. Memory (Journey) tracks learning against course outcomes across institutional, teacher, and student levels and is also at MVP.

Problem addressed

Live lectures allow few questions, while outside support does not scale and general AI tools lack course context. ArkJunior provides support during and after lectures while capturing student understanding through their interactions.

How it was built

ArkJunior was built in roughly five months by a six-person research and engineering team at X-Lab, with light HKU support on integration and security. It is designed to work within existing learning management systems through LTI. Two pilots ran ten days apart, allowing findings from the first to inform improvements before the second. The finished platform will also expose several functions as standalone services.

Cost & resources

Using existing engineering capacity, ArkJunior adds no build or licensing costs to the teaching budget. The first pilot cost under US$225 in AI and infrastructure for nine lectures and 140 students, including under US$65 for AI. Scaling to ten courses is projected at roughly US$4,900 within the existing operating budget.

Outcomes

Two pilots involving 190 students generated around 1,300 genuine questions. In the second pilot, 86% were conceptual and 47% challenged or extended the material. More than half of students said they asked ArkJunior questions they would hesitate to ask in class, while 77% valued asking questions mid-lecture. Answer quality scored 4.0/5.0. Students still preferred human lecturers but saw ArkJunior as a useful complement. They suggested using it for definitions and theory, while reserving discussion and contested material for teachers.

Risks

Students reported lower attention than with a human instructor, while deeper engagement was concentrated among a small group. The system sometimes presented the lecture’s implied position rather than both sides, and students wanted answers beyond the uploaded materials. The format remains untested in STEM and practical courses, while Tutoring, Memory, and per-student costs remain early or untested.
